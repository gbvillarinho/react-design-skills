# Examples: from subject to visual direction

These examples show the reasoning, not recipes. Don't copy the outputs: use the method of looking at the subject's materials, vocabulary and audience and letting the choices come from there. If an example matches your brief almost word for word, be suspicious and look for another reading of the subject.

| Subject | What its world offers | Possible direction |
|---|---|---|
| Neighborhood bakery | Flour, oven, brown-paper labels, chalkboards, bake times | Crust and dough colors, hand-drawn type on prices, a hero showing the next bake time instead of a generic photo |
| Dashboard for financial analysts | Density, comparison, fast scanning, tables | Priority to tabular numerals, rigorous alignment, color only for positive or negative change, no decoration |
| Toy for girls aged 8–11 | Collecting, trading, customizing, saturated color | Generous shapes, a cheerful and specific palette, interaction that lets the child build something on the first screen |
| Architecture studio | Plans, sections, scale, materials | A grid reminiscent of a technical drawing sheet, large images of the projects, restrained type so it doesn't compete with the work |
| Meditation app | Breath, rhythm, silence | Little text, slow motion tied to breathing, a single focus color, plenty of empty space |
| Hardware store | Weight, metal, measurements, catalog | The spec sheet as the central visual element, high contrast, product photos on a neutral background |

## How to reach a direction

1. List 5 to 10 concrete things from the subject's world: objects, materials, terms, gestures.
2. Pick one of them to be the memorable element of the hero.
3. Let palette and typography come from that element, not from a general taste.
4. Compare with what you would do for any other similar brief. If it comes out the same, go back to step 1.
